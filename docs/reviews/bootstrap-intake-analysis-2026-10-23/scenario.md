# WorkshopFlow product idea

This is a fictional product brief for a rehearsal. It describes product wishes, not approval to spend money or contact real customers. We run an independent equipment repair workshop with two branches. Staff currently switch between email, a spreadsheet of jobs, a paper diagnosis sheet, a stock book and accounting software. We lose the connection between what a customer asked for, what was diagnosed, what they approved and what we finally did. I want one coherent application, initially for staff, that can grow over several years. I am describing the whole vision because choices made early should leave room for the rest. I do not have a technical architecture or a list of feature specifications.

## Page 1 Customers equipment and reception

The reception desk needs a customer search and customer page, an equipment search and equipment page, and a job intake page. A customer can be an individual or a business. Businesses can have several contacts; the person dropping off an item is not necessarily its owner or the person who can approve a repair. A business might authorize a site manager to approve up to an agreed amount. For now staff record approvals that arrive by phone or email; a later portal could let customers approve online. I do not want that later portal to require us to invent a completely different ownership system.

Equipment has our own internal identity, a visible workshop reference and, when present, a manufacturer serial number. Serial numbers can be absent or incorrectly copied. Two jobs for the same drill should share its service history. A job reference must still work if a customer changes their email, a contact leaves an employer, equipment moves to another site, or reception corrects a typo. I do not know whether references should be consecutive, prefixed by branch, or something else. Customers should be able to quote a simple reference to staff.

Reception records the reported fault, condition on arrival, included accessories, photos and a promised contact date. Staff should see that an item is already in the workshop rather than unknowingly opening a second active job for it. Sometimes a customer returns after a completed repair and says it is the same fault. That should connect to the old job without rewriting what happened then. Staff need to correct mistaken links, with a visible explanation. Future barcode labels and a mobile camera would help, but neither is needed to begin using the system.

## Page 2 Diagnosis quotes and customer approval

A technician works through the reported fault and records findings, proposed labor, parts and alternatives. There may be a diagnosis fee even when the customer declines the repair. We need a diagnosis page and a quote page, and ideally the whole job page explains what happens next. Several technicians might contribute, but there should be one person accountable for the recommendation. We often discover a second issue after opening the equipment. A revised quote must not make an earlier customer approval appear to authorize the new amount.

Quotes have draft, issued, accepted, declined, expired and superseded stages in our current language. I am unsure whether expired should mean an automatic date transition or just that staff need to recheck availability and price. An issued quote needs to remain understandable later, including its descriptions, labor rate, parts prices and tax treatment. Our parts price list changes; a historical quote should not change with it. A technician may offer a cheaper option and a recommended option. Customers sometimes approve a subset or impose a ceiling; I do not yet know how much flexibility the first version needs.

The big journey in my head is that a customer brings in broken equipment, we investigate it, explain the options, get approval, obtain parts, repair it and return it with a service report and an invoice. It feels like one customer experience even though many people work on it. I cannot say whether that is one software feature or many. The biggest current pain is losing track of findings and approval, and accidentally doing more work than was agreed. I would like advice on where useful delivery should start.

We must distinguish permission to diagnose, permission to buy a part and permission to perform a repair. The customer may withdraw before work starts. After work has started, staff must explain what already happened and what can still be stopped. Managers can approve goodwill work and limited exceptions, but we should retain who made that decision and why.

## Page 3 Parts inventory and suppliers

A parts catalog page holds internal part identity, supplier numbers, description, compatibility and current indicative prices. Supplier numbers can differ for the same interchangeable part. We keep stock at each branch and transfer parts between them. A stock page should show on-hand, reserved and expected quantities separately. We must never promise the last available part to two jobs. A technician can discover that a received part is faulty or the wrong variant; recording a receipt is not the same as proving it is usable.

Parts requests may start while drafting a quote, but a purchase commitment normally follows customer approval. Staff sometimes buy a low-cost diagnostic part earlier with a manager's approval. We need a purchase request page, a supplier order page and receiving. A supplier order can serve several jobs, and a job can need parts from several suppliers. Suppliers sometimes split deliveries, substitute an item, change price or cancel. Customers should not see internal supplier margins. I do not want the repair job status to say ready just because the purchasing screen says received.

Initially staff can enter supplier orders and receipts by hand. Eventually I would like email order generation, supplier catalog imports, delivery tracking and perhaps electronic ordering. We have no supplier API agreements. If connectivity fails after an external order is sent, someone must be able to establish whether an order exists before resending it. The future integrations should have an owner, but I do not want to commission all of them at the outset.

Inventory adjustments need a reason. Consumption belongs to actual repair work and should be traceable to that job. Returns to stock, returns to a supplier and scrap are different. I do not yet know whether detailed batch tracking is necessary; serial tracking for high-value replacement units might become necessary later. Whatever we do first should not confuse a reserved part with a consumed part.

## Page 4 Scheduling repair and delivery

A planner sees jobs waiting for diagnosis, approval, parts or technician capacity. Each branch has its own technicians, working hours and workstations. A technician may work at both branches. A scheduling board should suggest where a job can fit and make promises visible, but I do not need automatic optimization initially. Emergency jobs can interrupt the plan. Moving a job should not silently change a promised customer date.

Work has a checklist, actual labor entries, parts used, observations and a completion review. A technician should be able to pause for a newly discovered issue without losing work. Some operations need another person's safety check. Our job language is received, diagnosing, waiting for approval, approved, waiting for parts, scheduled, in progress, ready for collection, collected and closed. In reality these overlap: some parts may arrive while approval is still pending, and work may be split across technicians. I suspect a single long status list might be misleading, but I need help translating that into a model.

When a repair fails its final test we need to return to work while preserving the failed test and previous customer communications. A service report explains the reported fault, work done, parts replaced and relevant safety advice. It should identify the equipment and job, and retain the meaning of the report as handed over. We must not overwrite an earlier report just because somebody later corrects an internal note.

The collection desk sees whether the item is ready, who can collect it, what accessories should go back and what financial conditions remain. Sometimes a courier collects for a business. We may transfer a completed item to the other branch for collection. The system must not pretend the equipment is at both branches. Offline technician work is a future possibility, but online use in both workshops is acceptable initially. Staff normally use desktop browsers, with a few tablets on benches.

## Page 5 Money history and warranty

An invoice page should turn approved work and actual authorized changes into something accounting can use. Our accounting package remains the financial authority in the first version. We have not selected an integration and do not want a second general ledger. Initially a staff member can record an accounting reference. Later we would like an export and payment-status import. Failed exports must not produce duplicate invoices. A corrected invoice is different from editing an internal repair estimate.

Private customers generally pay before collection, whereas approved business accounts can have credit terms. Managers can authorize exceptions. A refunded charge, a credit note and a goodwill repair are different situations. We need enough separation that adding online payments later does not make the repair team responsible for card data. I have no payment-provider preference, approved production budget or customer-facing hosting commitment.

The equipment history page should let staff understand previous faults, diagnoses, repairs, reports and warranty cases. A warranty claim can refer to a previous job but may cover only part of the new work. A customer may have sold the equipment since the earlier repair; the new owner should not automatically receive the old owner's personal information. We need to understand whether a warranty follows the equipment, the paying customer or the particular repair. I have not decided those policies.

Recurring maintenance is a future idea: a business customer maintains a fleet and wants reminders, a service calendar and perhaps a contract price. Some visits happen on site, which could introduce location, travel and mobile requirements. I want the initial equipment and job identities to survive that evolution. I do not need the complete contract billing and reminder behavior decided now. Historical work must remain explainable if a maintenance policy changes.

## Page 6 Administration reporting and future access

Managers need a staff page, branch settings, rates and permissions, and a dashboard of work waiting on people, customers, parts and money. A receptionist should not change technician safety results. Technicians should see the customer information needed for the job, without automatically gaining every branch's financial reports. Managers need cross-branch oversight. We are one company with two branches today, not a marketplace of independent repair businesses. A franchise version is only a possible future business idea.

I want customers eventually to look up their jobs, accept quotes and download their own service reports. A business contact might act for several sites and a private customer might own several items. Invitations and revoked permissions should be deliberate. A future supplier portal should expose supplier-related facts without giving suppliers customer records. These future actors seem relevant to identity and data ownership now, even if their screens are later deliveries.

For the user experience, prioritize clear next actions, visible reasons for waiting, readable tables and keyboard use. We do not have a brand guide, and a sensible provisional visual baseline is fine. Both branches need to use the application simultaneously without silently overwriting each other's work. Retaining commercial approval and service-history evidence matters more than elaborate dashboards. We have no exact availability or response-time commitment yet; production deployment and retention policy require later decisions before real use.

I expect seven broad areas of user work: receiving equipment, diagnosing and agreeing work, obtaining parts, planning and executing repairs, billing and collection, service history and recurring care, and administration with later external access. Those names describe my vision, not technical boundaries. Please preserve details and future ideas that affect relationships, and help me understand a sensible order of useful deliverables. A large first delivery that tries to do all seven areas would be hard for us to review.
